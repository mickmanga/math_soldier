Complete Course on Vectors

Vectors are fundamental mathematical objects used in many fields: mathematics, physics, computer science, etc. This course covers the definition, graphical representation, and the main operations on two-dimensional vectors, along with a more in-depth look at norm, direction, projection, and normalization.

1. General Definition

In two dimensions, a vector is defined by two main attributes:

  • Direction: The orientation of the vector in the plane.
  • Magnitude (or norm): The length of the vector, usually representing the intensity or force associated with it.

We commonly denote a vector V in the form (x, y), where x and y are its components along the X and Y axes.

2. Graphical Representation and Components

In a two-dimensional plane, the vector (x, y) can be represented by an arrow starting at the origin ((0, 0)) and ending at the point (x, y). The direction of this arrow corresponds to the vector's orientation, and its length corresponds to the vector's magnitude.

3. Magnitude and Direction

The magnitude (or norm) of a vector V = (x, y) is calculated using the Pythagorean theorem:

||V|| = √(x² + y²)

The direction of a vector, often expressed as an angle (in degrees or radians), is generally determined by:

θ = arctan(y / x)

Example: For V = (3, 4):

Magnitude: ||V|| = √(3² + 4²) = 5
Angle: θ = arctan(4/3) ≈ 53.13°

4. Addition and Subtraction of Vectors

Operations between vectors are done component-wise.

4.1 Addition

For two vectors A = (a₁, a₂) and B = (b₁, b₂):

A + B = (a₁ + b₁, a₂ + b₂)

Example: A = (2, 3), B = (4, 1):

A + B = (2 + 4, 3 + 1) = (6, 4)

4.2 Subtraction

For subtraction, just subtract the corresponding components:

A - B = (a₁ - b₁, a₂ - b₂)

Example: A = (2, 3), B = (1, -2):

A - B = (2 - 1, 3 - (-2)) = (1, 5)

5. Scalar Multiplication

Multiplying a vector by a scalar k enlarges or shrinks its magnitude without changing its direction (unless k is negative, in which case the direction is reversed).

k × V = (k × x, k × y)

Example: V = (3, 4), k = 2:

2 × V = (6, 8)

6. Dot Product

The dot product of two vectors A = (a₁, a₂) and B = (b₁, b₂) is defined as:

A · B = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂

It can also be expressed in terms of the norms of the vectors and the angle θ between them:

A · B = ||A|| · ||B|| · cos(θ)

The dot product is useful for:

  • Determining if two vectors are orthogonal (the dot product is 0).
  • Calculating the angle between two vectors.
  • Performing projection operations in physics and geometry.

7. Normalization, Projection, and Conclusion

7.1 Unit Vector and Normalization

A unit vector is a vector with a magnitude of 1. For any non-zero vector V, you can normalize it (convert it to a unit vector) by dividing it by its own norm:

Vunit = V / ||V||

This operation is very common when you want to keep only the direction of a vector, discarding its scale.

7.2 Projection of a Vector

The projection of a vector A onto another vector B finds the component of A in the direction of B. It is often written as:

projB(A) = ( (A · B) / ||B||² ) · B

Projection is particularly useful for decomposing forces in physics or extracting a component along a given axis.

7.3 Conclusion

Vectors form an essential basis for describing and understanding geometric and physical phenomena. In two dimensions, their manipulation (addition, subtraction, dot product, normalization, etc.) provides powerful tools to model translations, directions, and even dynamic phenomena.

Building on this knowledge, you can explore:

  • Extending vectors to three dimensions (3D).
  • The cross product, crucial in 3D for calculating normals.
  • More advanced linear algebra, including matrices and linear transformations.

Mastering these fundamental concepts opens the door to a wide range of applications in science, engineering, and beyond.
